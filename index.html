01 · Who holds the pie

Seven slices of the population

How to read this chart

Each coloured band is one slice of the adult population, from the poorest half at the bottom to the richest 0.001% at the top. Its height is that slice's share of the whole pie. The slices never overlap and always add up to 100%. Grey shading marks years that the World Inequality Database itself flags as less reliable (darker means less reliable). For wealth, the dashed line in 1988 is when WID starts adjusting its top group to Forbes billionaire figures. The two solid lines are the period you picked.

The richest slices hold very few people, so their bands are thin. Each one gets its own scale below.

02 · How each slice changed

A bigger pie, cut differently

How to read this chart

Every slice changes for two reasons. First, the whole pie grows: grey bars show what that alone would have added, at the slice's average share of the period. Second, the slice's share moves: blue bars when it got a bigger share, red when smaller. Grey plus colour is the slice's real change, written at the end of each row. These are slices of a ranking, not fixed groups of people: someone can move from one slice to another over time, so this is not money taken from one group and given to another.

03 · Real wealth per person

Everyone's slice, in euros

How to read this chart

The average per adult inside each slice, adjusted for inflation. The vertical scale multiplies by ten at each step, so a line that climbs at the same slope as another grows at the same pace, however far apart they are. A slice can lose share of the pie while its members still get richer, because the pie itself grows.

04 · Growth, rank by rank

Who grew fastest?

For experts: growth of each of 127 ranks
How to read this chart

Average yearly growth, after inflation, for each of 127 ranks of the population, from the poorest 1% on the left to the richest 0.001% on the right. The right end zooms in: the last 28 points are all inside the top 1%. The flat line is the growth of the average. It compares ranks, not the same people. This chart always uses WID as published.

05 · How sure is the top?

530 people, several estimates

Why the top is hard. Surveys rarely reach billionaires, and much of their income stays inside their companies. WID estimates the top 0.001% from tax and national-accounts data and, since 1988, raises it to at least the Forbes billionaire figures. Rich lists such as Challenges and Forbes value fortunes from company prices, but count families or citizens rather than adults living in France.

06 · Top incomes

Company profits that stay inside firms

For the largest fortunes, much income can stay inside the companies they control as retained profits (the Institut des politiques publiques, IPP, shows this for the 75 richest tax households in 2016). IPP linked French firms to their owners and measured top incomes several ways. Amounts are in billions of 2016 euros for each group. Groups differ slightly: WID counts adults, IPP counts tax households.

09 · Method and limits

How it is computed, and what it cannot say

  1. Backbone. The World Inequality Database (WID.world) splits all of France's national income and household wealth among adults, combining tax records, surveys and national accounts (Garbinti, Goupille-Lebret & Piketty 2018, 2021).
  2. Three pies. Wealth: housing, business and financial assets minus debts. Income before tax: after pensions, before taxes; it includes non-cash items such as owners' imputed rent and company profits attributed to shareholders. Income after tax & public services: all taxes removed, cash benefits added, and public spending attributed to people; health counts about the same per person, education and other spending in proportion to income (Bozio et al. 2024; the French series come from Fisher-Post & Gethin 2023; since 2019 they repeat the 2019 tax-and-transfer ratios).
  3. Slices. Seven groups that partition the adult population, each summed from WID's 127 ranks and checked against WID's own published shares.
  4. Real euros. WID's national-income price index, or INSEE's consumer prices (from 1950).
  5. Change of a slice. Growth effect (the average share times the growth of the pie) plus share effect (the change in share times the average size of the pie). The split is exact.
  6. The very top. WID raises its top group to Forbes totals from 1988. Expert settings swap it for alternatives in both directions: the ECB / Banque de France level (lower) or the Challenges rich list under assumptions inspired by Garbinti, Goupille-Lebret & Piketty's data appendix (higher).
  7. Housing. Years of cash income and yearly repayment for a 70 m² flat, by income group of INSEE's survey.

Limits

  • Per adult and split equally within couples: not comparable in level with INSEE's niveau de vie.
  • Old and recent years use different methods: estate records before 1970, tax data 1970–2014, an extrapolation after 2014. Single years can be odd (for example 1975 and 1984 for the top 0.001%).
  • The population aged 20+ doubled since 1900 and grew much older, which moves wealth shares by itself.
  • One national price index for everyone; prices are about 7% higher in the Paris urban area.
  • The Challenges and Forbes figures are quoted from published articles and can differ by a few percent between sources.
10 · Glossary

Words used on this page

Adult, equal split
Everyone aged 20 or over; a couple's income and wealth are divided half and half. Children are not counted.
Slice (layer)
A group of adults defined by rank, for example the richest 1% to 0.1%. The seven slices do not overlap.
Top 0.001%
The richest one adult in 100,000: about 530 people in France today.
Share of the pie
The part of all wealth (or income) that a slice holds.
Growth effect, share effect
The two parts of a slice's change: the pie growing, and the slice's share moving.
Net wealth
Housing, business and financial assets minus debts.
Income after tax and public services
Income after all taxes, plus cash benefits, plus the value of public services such as health care and schools.
Distributional national accounts
Estimates that split the whole of national income and wealth across the population, so the slices add up to the national totals.
Rich list
A magazine ranking of the largest fortunes (Challenges' 500 families, Forbes billionaires).
Calibration to Forbes
WID raises its top group so that it holds at least what Forbes counts for French billionaires.
Extrapolated year
A year WID estimates by extending earlier data, to be revised when new tax data arrive.
Consumption unit
INSEE's way to compare households of different sizes: 1 for the first adult, 0.5 per other person aged 14+, 0.3 per child.
11 · Sources and review

Check everything

This analysis was built with the help of Claude, an AI model by Anthropic, under the maintainer's direction. All code, data and documentation are public, with the reviews of this work: four adversarial reviews and a second round, each run by a separate AI reviewer agent (Claude) with a critical brief. They are not peer review by outside experts, and since author and reviewers share a model they may share blind spots; human reviewers are invited through the repository.
